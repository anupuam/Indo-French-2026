# Methodology Summary and Justification for Collaboration

## 1. Summary of Proposed Methodology (~1000 words)

This project proposes a Multi-Level Optimization (MLO) framework for intelligent wearable robotic assistance to mobility-impaired and dependent individuals. The central idea is that optimization—implemented through evolutionary, metaheuristic, and transport-theoretic methods—serves as the unifying architectural principle across every functional layer of the assistive system. Rather than treating perception, trajectory generation, and control as isolated engineering problems, the MLO framework connects them into a coherent, continuously self-tuning pipeline in which improvements at one level systematically enhance the performance of adjacent levels.

**Overall Architecture**

The framework is organized into three hierarchical levels. The high level is responsible for perceiving the user's gait state and locomotion intent from body-worn sensors. The mid level translates that perceptual understanding into personalized, biomechanically appropriate assistive trajectories. The low level delivers the resulting motion commands as precise, safe, and stable joint torques through the exoskeleton actuators. A supervisory meta-optimizer monitors system-wide performance and adjusts the operating parameters of all three levels in response to evolving user states, environmental conditions, and task demands. These levels communicate through a shared real-time data bus, ensuring that perceptual context flows downward to inform trajectory and control decisions, while control-level feedback flows upward to trigger perceptual re-evaluation when unexpected perturbations are detected.

**High-Level Perception and Intention Recognition**

The sensing subsystem consists of miniature inertial measurement units placed on the lower limbs and trunk, combined with flexible plantar pressure insoles beneath each foot, capturing joint kinematics and ground interaction forces in real time. The raw multivariate sensor streams are fed into a hybrid deep learning model combining three stages: a fuzzy feature extraction layer that transforms raw signals through parameterized membership functions to capture the inherent imprecision of biomechanical data; one-dimensional convolutional layers that extract local temporal patterns; and gated recurrent unit layers that model long-range temporal dependencies across the gait cycle. The output is a probabilistic classification over eight standard gait phases.

Rather than manually designing this neural architecture, the project employs an evolutionary neural architecture search strategy. A population of candidate architectures is evolved over multiple generations using a multi-objective approach that balances classification accuracy against computational cost through Pareto-optimal selection, yielding architectures suited to the target embedded hardware. Training hyperparameters of elite architectures are further refined using particle swarm optimization, automating the entire model design and tuning process.

To address gait variability within and across individuals, the high level includes an intra-subject variability modelling component. Gait cycles are clustered into variability modes using a Gaussian mixture model updated online through Bayesian inference. This enables the system to anticipate gait transitions—such as shifting from level walking to stair climbing, or compensating for fatigue—and proactively adjust downstream parameters before the transition fully unfolds.

**Mid-Level Adaptive Trajectory Generation**

The mid level produces personalized reference trajectories for the exoskeleton using Optimal Transport theory for domain adaptation. A persistent challenge in assistive robotics is that trajectory models trained on one population do not generalize well to new individuals with different body proportions, pathologies, or walking habits. Optimal Transport provides a mathematically principled way to align the gait distribution of a new user with those of previously characterized reference subjects.

The method computes a Wasserstein barycenter—a weighted average in the space of probability distributions—across a library of reference subjects, with weights determined by anthropometric similarity to the new user. The Sinkhorn algorithm provides computationally efficient entropic regularization. When sufficient user-specific data become available, the template is refined through online fine-tuning. A concept drift monitor based on statistical divergence tests detects when the user's gait distribution shifts over time due to fatigue, recovery, or environmental change, and triggers re-estimation of the transport map.

The mid level also estimates normalized vertical ground reaction forces from plantar pressure and inertial data, providing the canonical biomechanical signature of gait sub-phases used for sub-phase detection and trajectory generation. A lightweight backup model enables graceful degradation when plantar sensors are temporarily unavailable.

**Low-Level Robust Adaptive Control**

The low level ensures that the personalized reference trajectories are faithfully and safely tracked by the exoskeleton joints. The control design is based on Active Disturbance Rejection Control, a paradigm that treats all model uncertainties, unmodeled dynamics, external perturbations, and the unknown human joint torque as a single lumped disturbance, which is estimated in real time by an extended state observer. This approach avoids the need for precise dynamic models of the coupled human-exoskeleton system—a significant practical advantage, since such models are inherently uncertain and vary across users.

To prevent the well-known problem of parameter drift in adaptive systems operating under persistent bounded excitation (as occurs during repetitive walking), the observer bandwidth is updated through a sigma-modification adaptive law that includes a leakage term guaranteeing bounded parameter evolution. The resulting controller—termed sigma-modified Adaptive Active Disturbance Rejection Control—is formally proven to achieve uniformly ultimately bounded tracking error through Lyapunov stability analysis, providing rigorous safety guarantees that are essential for deployment with vulnerable user populations.

The control parameters—including observer and controller gains—are not fixed but are continuously tuned online using a computationally lightweight Differential Evolution optimizer operating on a sliding window of recent tracking data. The cost function balances tracking accuracy, torque economy (as a metabolic cost surrogate), and actuator saturation avoidance. This metaheuristic tuning enables the controller to adapt in real time to changing gait speeds, terrain conditions, and user-specific biomechanical characteristics without manual recalibration.

**System Integration and Validation**

The three levels are integrated into a hierarchical real-time control architecture operating at differentiated update rates: perception at fifty hertz, trajectory generation at one hundred hertz, and torque control at one thousand hertz. Inter-level synergy is a defining feature: the variability model from the high level modulates the trajectory generator's conservatism; the trajectory generator provides feedforward torque estimates to ease the controller's disturbance estimation burden; and high tracking errors at the low level trigger elevated perceptual sampling and perturbation recovery modes. The integrated system is validated through progressively challenging scenarios—from level ground walking and terrain transitions through speed variations, perturbation recovery, and extended daily use sessions with elderly and post-stroke participants—at laboratories in both India and France.


## 2. Justification for Collaboration

The proposed Indo-French collaboration is not merely convenient but structurally essential to the scientific and translational objectives of this project. The two research teams bring deeply complementary expertise, infrastructure, and demographic access that neither could replicate independently, and their combination creates a research capability qualitatively greater than the sum of its parts.

**Complementarity of Scientific Expertise.** The Indian Principal Investigator's research group at the Department of Mathematics and Computing, Dr B.R. Ambedkar NIT Jalandhar, has established expertise in evolutionary computation, metaheuristic optimization, and machine learning, with a strong publication record in journals such as Swarm and Evolutionary Computation, Applied Soft Computing, Engineering Applications of Artificial Intelligence, and Knowledge-Based Systems. This group provides the algorithmic foundation for the high-level perception module—including the evolutionary neural architecture search, particle swarm optimization-based hyperparameter tuning, and the Gaussian mixture model-based intra-subject variability framework—as well as the Differential Evolution-based online tuning of the low-level controller. The French Principal Investigator's group brings deep competence in wearable robotics, human motion analysis, gait biomechanics, adaptive control of lower-limb exoskeletons, and clinical experimental methodology, with publications in IEEE Transactions on Robotics, IEEE Transactions on Automation Science and Engineering, and IEEE Robotics and Automation Letters. This group provides the physical exoskeleton platform, the clinical testing infrastructure, the adaptive control design (including the sigma-modified ADRC framework and Lyapunov stability analysis), and direct access to clinical populations through affiliated rehabilitation hospitals. Neither team possesses the full spectrum of skills required: the Indian team lacks access to physical exoskeleton hardware and clinical populations, while the French team lacks the depth in evolutionary optimization and neural architecture search that is central to the project's novelty.

**Complementarity of Infrastructure.** The Indian site provides a motion capture laboratory with high-speed cameras and force plates for gold-standard gait analysis, high-performance GPU workstations for large-scale neural architecture search experiments, and access to a large pool of healthy adult participants for data collection. The French site provides a fully instrumented lower-limb exoskeleton platform for hardware-in-the-loop and real-world testing, along with clinical recruitment pathways to elderly and post-stroke participants through affiliated rehabilitation hospitals. The combined infrastructure enables a complete development-to-validation pipeline that spans algorithmic development, simulation, hardware integration, healthy-subject testing, and clinical validation—a pipeline that no single institution in either country could provide alone.

**Demographic and Population Diversity.** A critical scientific contribution of this project is the creation of the Indo-French Gait Variability Dataset, which will include recordings from both Indian and French/European populations. This cross-continental dataset addresses a major limitation of existing assistive robotics research, which is overwhelmingly based on Western European and North American cohorts and therefore fails to account for anthropometric, biomechanical, and neuromuscular differences across global populations. The Optimal Transport-based domain adaptation framework is specifically designed to handle such inter-population variability, and its validation requires data from demographically distinct groups—a requirement that the Indo-French partnership uniquely satisfies.

**Mutual Societal Need.** Both India and France face urgent demographic pressures that make this research directly relevant to national policy. India has over 26 million persons with disabilities (20 percent with locomotor impairments), a projected elderly population of 319 million by 2050, and a severe shortfall of rehabilitation professionals. France, as part of the European Union's ageing demographic, faces similar challenges and has prioritized assistive technologies through Horizon Europe. The collaboration ensures that the resulting technologies are validated for both population contexts and can be translated through both national innovation ecosystems—leveraging India's cost-competitive manufacturing capability and France's established regulatory and clinical trial frameworks.

**Knowledge Transfer and Capacity Building.** The project includes a structured programme of researcher exchanges (a minimum of four visits per year in each direction), joint supervision of doctoral students, and a planned Indo-French summer school on Optimization and AI for Assistive Robotics. These activities build lasting human capital networks that extend well beyond the project lifetime, creating a cadre of researchers fluent in both evolutionary computation and experimental wearable robotics—a combination that is rare globally and strategically valuable for both countries' research ecosystems.

**Inability to Achieve Objectives Independently.** The central hypothesis of this proposal—that embedding principled optimization at every functional level of a wearable robotic system creates a synergistic, self-tuning architecture superior to single-level designs—can only be tested through a fully integrated system that spans algorithmic design, physical prototyping, and clinical validation with diverse populations. This integration is structurally impossible for either team working alone. The Indian team can develop and validate algorithms in simulation but cannot test them on physical exoskeletons or with clinical populations. The French team can build and test robotic systems but cannot independently develop the evolutionary optimization and neural architecture search frameworks that constitute the project's core scientific novelty. The collaboration is therefore not an optional enhancement but a structural prerequisite for achieving the project's objectives.
